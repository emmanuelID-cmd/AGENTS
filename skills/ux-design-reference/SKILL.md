# UX Design Reference

Use this skill whenever a task creates, changes, audits, or reviews a user-facing experience, including screens, flows, navigation, forms, dashboards, tables, search, settings, empty states, error states, and responsive behavior.

This skill is a practical interpretation of the Laws of UX reference at https://lawsofux.com/. Consult the source for its current catalog and examples. Do not copy the site's text, illustrations, or other protected content wholesale.

## Required review method

1. Identify the users, context, primary task, constraints, and highest-risk decision.
2. Map a small set of relevant UX principles to concrete design choices.
3. Check entry, orientation, action, feedback, recovery, completion, and return use.
4. Record trade-offs and evidence. Principles prompt investigation; they do not replace user research, accessibility checks, or product requirements.

## Practical principles

- Reduce choice overload with grouping, clear defaults, and progressive disclosure.
- Match effort to importance; make frequent actions easy and destructive actions deliberate.
- Preserve recognition and continuity with familiar patterns, stable labels, visible status, and retained input.
- Create clear hierarchy and proximity; never rely on color alone.
- Provide usable targets, spacing, keyboard access, and responsive behavior.
- Make loading, success, empty, unavailable, and error states immediate and actionable.
- Show relevant instructions and context at the point of need.
- Prevent likely mistakes, explain failures plainly, preserve valid work, and provide undo or safe retry where possible.
- Follow platform conventions unless a deviation has a clear, explained benefit.
- Optimize for task comprehension, completion, error rate, time, and satisfaction—not novelty alone.

## Deliverable expectations

For a design proposal or review, include the primary user and task; relevant principles and the decision each supports; accessibility, responsive, loading, empty, error, and recovery states; assumptions and validation steps; and intentional trade-offs.

## Boundaries

Do not treat UX laws as universal proof, replace accessibility standards, invent research, or prescribe a visual style without product context. Preserve existing design tokens and conventions unless approved work explicitly changes them.
